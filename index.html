Demand Driven Material Requirements Planning

Position. Protect.
Pull. Adapt.

DDMRP is a formal multi-echelon planning and execution method. Instead of netting a forecast through every BOM level, it places stock buffers at a few strategic decoupling points and replenishes them from actual demand.

Each buffer is sized from average daily usage and decoupled lead time, and supply is triggered when the net flow position drops into the yellow zone.

Why DDMRP

The problem it solves

Conventional MRP nets a forecast through every BOM level and recalculates every order on every run. Ptak & Smith describe four symptoms that follow (summarised from knowledge of the book).

MRP nervousness

Small changes in forecast or orders ripple through every level. Each run reschedules many orders, planners stop trusting the messages and work around the system in spreadsheets.

Bullwhip effect

Each tier plans from the orders of the tier below, not from real demand. Forecast error and batching grow at every step, so suppliers see far more variability than end customers create.

Bimodal inventory

Too much of what is not needed and too little of what is, at the same time: high inventory and poor service together. See the distribution chart in the three views.

Flow first

DDI builds on Plossl's first law of manufacturing: the benefits depend on how fast relevant information and materials flow. Decoupling points and actual-demand signals protect that flow.

Why now: the new normal

Planning got harder while the tools stayed the same. Supply and demand cross the world; products live for months, not decades; customers wait days, not weeks; there are many more variants and options; long lead time parts are common; forecasts miss; and there is pressure to hold less stock. The past is a weaker guide to the future than it used to be.

Bullwhip, illustrated

Small ups and downs in customer demand become big swings further up the chain

  • Customers buy about 20 a day, with small random ups and downs (blue line).
  • The retailer, distributor and plant never see those customers. Each one only sees the orders from the company below it, guesses future demand from them, and orders enough to cover its lead time.
  • When orders tick up, each company orders extra, just in case; when they dip, it orders less. These over-reactions add up, so the further from the customer, the wilder the orders (red lines).
  • How to read ×: × 5.4 means the plant's orders swing 5.4 times as much as what customers actually buy.
  • Why it matters for DDMRP: a decoupling point breaks this chain. Its buffer is refilled from actual consumption (net flow), not from guesses based on the orders below.

The six components

The Demand Driven Institute describes six components in a cycle. Components 1–3 are the Master Settings, 4–5 run every day, and 6 adapts the Master Settings over time. Older descriptions list five components and treat the adaptation step separately.

  1. Position1Strategic decouplingDecide where to place decoupling points, so that lead times compress and variability stops travelling through the network.
  2. Protect2Buffer profiles and levelsSize each buffer's red, yellow and green zones from ADU, decoupled lead time and its buffer profile.
  3. Protect3Dynamic buffer adjustmentsRecalculate zones as usage changes, and plan factor adjustments for seasonality, ramps and known events.
  4. Pull4Demand-driven planningGenerate supply from the net flow equation: order up to top of green when net flow is in yellow or red.
  5. Pull5Visible and collaborative executionManage open orders by buffer status alerts and synchronization alerts, not by due dates alone.
  6. Adapt6Tactical adaptationReview past performance and planned activity, then adjust the Master Settings: decoupling points, buffer profiles and adjustments.

Component 1

Strategic decoupling

A decoupling point is an inventory position that separates supply variability from demand variability. Between decoupling points, planning and execution run independently, so the bullwhip effect is dampened and the lead time the customer sees is the decoupled lead time, not the full cumulative lead time.

Six positioning criteria

1Customer tolerance time
How long a typical customer will wait before looking elsewhere. Anything the customer won't wait for has to come from a buffer.
Ask: how fast must we deliver?
2Market potential lead time
A shorter lead time that would let you charge more or win business you lose today.
Ask: would being faster pay?
3Sales order visibility horizon
How far ahead real orders are known. Work inside this window can be made to order; work beyond it has to be anticipated.
Ask: how far ahead do we see actual demand?
4External variability
Where demand swings and spikes come in from customers, and where supply disruptions come in from suppliers.
Ask: where does variability enter the system?
5Inventory leverage and flexibility
Points in the bill of material or distribution network where one buffer serves many parents or locations, such as common components and divergence points.
Ask: where does one buffer protect the most?
6Critical operation protection
Operations with limited capacity, or where quality problems could stop the flow, should not run short of material.
Ask: which resource must never starve?

What a decoupling point changes

  • Lead time compresses: items above the buffer only count the lead time down to it (the DLT), not the full cumulative lead time.
  • Variability stops spreading: upstream sees steady replenishment orders instead of every swing in customer demand (less bullwhip).
  • Horizons separate: each segment between decoupling points is planned and scheduled on its own.

Criteria and effects paraphrased from knowledge of Ptak & Smith (strategic inventory positioning); check the exact wording in the book.

Matrix BOM and ASR lead time

Component 2

Buffer profiles and levels

A buffer profile groups parts that behave alike: item type (make, buy, distributed, intermediate), lead time category and variability category. The profile supplies two factors. Zones are then sized from average daily usage (ADU) and decoupled lead time (DLT).

Green

Green = max(ADU × DLT × LTF, ADU × DOC, MOQ)

Order frequency and typical order size. The largest of the lead-time-based amount, the desired order cycle and the minimum order quantity.

Yellow

Yellow = ADU × DLT

Demand coverage over the decoupled lead time: the heart of the buffer.

Red

Red base = ADU × DLT × LTF
Red safety = Red base × VF
Red = Red base + Red safety

Embedded safety, scaled up for variability.

Buffer types, and parts that are not buffered

Replenished

The standard buffer: zones recalculated from ADU, DLT and the buffer profile; supply is generated when NFP ≤ TOY.

Replenished override

Zones set to fixed values instead of being calculated, for example because of a contract, a container size or limited space.

Min-max

A simpler two-level buffer: reorder at a minimum, order up to a maximum. Used for low-impact items.

Non-replenished

Stock that is used up but not resupplied: a last-time buy, a one-off lot or the remainder of a phase-out.

Lead-time-managed part

A long lead time part that is not buffered. Its open orders are followed with lead time alerts (component 5).

Non-buffered part

Planned by decoupled explosion from a buffered parent (component 4); its timing is followed with material synchronization alerts.

Type names and uses are from knowledge of Ptak & Smith; verify the min-max trigger rule against the book.

Buffer profiles in detail

Profile codes, the profile grid, and one part under different profiles

  • Profile code: item type (P purchased, M manufactured, D distributed, I intermediate) + lead time category (S, M, L) + variability category (L, M, H). For example MLH = manufactured, long lead time, high variability. Four item types × three lead time × three variability categories give 36 profiles.
  • Factors: the lead time category sets the LTF, the variability category the VF. The item type doesn't change the maths here; it groups parts so each group can get its own factor values.
  • Red and green can use different LTFs: the LTF for the red base and the LTF for green don't have to be the same. Tick the box to give green its own.
  • Profile assignment matters: the same part (same ADU, DLT, MOQ) gets a very different buffer, average stock and order frequency under another profile. Click codes in the grid to compare up to four.

What a buffer tells you before any order is placed

Planning · Execution · Analytics

Three views of a buffer

DDMRP keeps a careful line between planning and execution. Planning works on the net flow position and ends when an order recommendation becomes an open order. Execution then manages those open orders against on-hand stock. Analytics looks back over time to check whether buffers are sized right, and feeds dynamic adjustments and tactical adaptation. Component 3

Dynamic buffer adjustments

Buffers are not static safety stock. They flex with the business in three ways.

Recalculated adjustments
Zones are recalculated as rolling ADU (and DLT or MOQ) changes.
Planned adjustments
Factors applied for a period: seasonality, promotions, product introductions and phase-outs.
Promotions
A campaign runs for weeks and changes demand: give it a DAF before, during and maybe after. An event is a one-off that doesn't change ADU: enter it as an internal order so it shows up as a spike (see qualified demand in component 4), and cancel it on its due date so it never sits as past-due demand.
Manual adjustments
Planner overrides for known situations the data can't see.

Planned adjustment factors

Adjusted ADU = ADU × DAF

Demand adjustment factor: shifts the whole buffer for ramps up or down. Typically staggered across a BOM by lead time.

Zoneadj = Zone × ZAF

Zone adjustment factor: changes one zone, for example a larger green zone for a supplier with long changeovers.

DLTadj = DLT × LTAF

Lead time adjustment factor: covers a known lead time change, for example a supplier shutdown.

Recalculated adjustments · week by week

Zones follow ADU, lead time, profile and MOQ changes

  • Every week the zones are recalculated from that week's inputs. While ADU climbs and settles, all three zones move with it.
  • MOQ floor: with an MOQ from week 1, green stays flat at the MOQ while the lead-time green is smaller; red and yellow still move.
  • Lead time cut: a shorter lead time shrinks yellow (ADU × DLT) and moves the part into another lead time category, so the LTF changes too.
  • MOQ added later: green jumps to the new MOQ the week it applies.

Planned adjustment · strong seasonality

ADU window, supply offset and a capacity cap

  • ADU window and type: a long past-looking ADU trails the season, too low on the way up and too high on the way down. A shorter, blended or forward-looking ADU follows it more closely, and the DAF covers what is left.
  • Capacity: if the peak is above what the plant can make, the extra has to be made earlier. The DAF is then pulled forward to the week the build-ahead starts, to level the load.
  • Supply offset: components need their DAF earlier still, by their cumulative lead time, unless their own buffers can take the swing.

Component 4

Demand-driven planning

Every day, each buffer's net flow position is compared with its zones. Only qualified demand counts, so normal forecast noise does not drive supply.

NFP = On-hand + On-order − Qualified demand

Qualified demand = past due + due today + qualified spikes

Order spike threshold (OST)
A day whose total demand is above this quantity is a spike. By default 50 % of the total red zone; the red base or a multiple of ADU are alternatives.
Order spike horizon (OSH)
How far ahead spikes are qualified, often one DLT. A long-range horizon (about the cumulative lead time) with its own threshold can catch large known orders further out.
Decoupled explosion
Supply orders explode requirements down the BOM only to the next decoupling point.

Planning rule

If NFP ≤ TOY → order quantity = TOG − NFP

The quantity is then rounded to order multiples or the minimum order quantity. The due date is today plus DLT.

Planning priority = NFP ÷ TOG (%)

The lower the percentage, the higher the priority. Colour follows the zone the NFP is in: red, yellow or green.

Supply order generation in detail

Decoupled explosion and order sizing

  • Explosion: a supply order for a buffered item passes its requirements down the BOM only until the next buffered component; there it becomes demand on that buffer and stops.
  • Dates: the explosion starts when the NFP falls to TOY or below and the order is due today + DLT. Each item is needed when its parent starts and released its own lead time earlier; the table compares this with a full MRP explosion of the same order, which runs through the buffers and ends up with releases in the past.
  • Can MRP decouple too? Only partly. Leftover stock stops an explosion by chance, not by plan (MRP aims to net to zero). Safety stock doesn't absorb variation; touching it raises expedites. A stop-explosion setting ends the explosion but nothing restarts it further down. Several master schedule levels can stop and restart it, but are hard to keep up and don't use the net flow equation [DDI concept, own words].
  • Order sizing bar: grey = today's net flow position, green = TOG − NFP, yellow = extra to reach the minimum order quantity, blue = extra from rounding up to the order multiple.
  • If NFP is above TOY (116), no order is placed. Rounding can push NFP above TOG; the last result shows by how much.
  • The buffer is the worked example (TOY 116, TOG 140).

Qualified demand in detail

Three ways to set the spike threshold, and a long-range horizon

  • Same order book, three thresholds: 50 % of the total red zone (the default), the red base, or a multiple of ADU. A lower threshold lets more days qualify, pulls the NFP down and orders earlier and more; a higher one ignores more of the order book.
  • Judged per day: each day's total counts, not single order lines (day +6 has two lines, 70 and 50).
  • Long-range horizon: some items get large, known orders far out (a seasonal event, a project). A second horizon of about the cumulative lead time, with its own higher threshold, pulls such an order into the NFP early enough to buy the components behind it. Only that order qualifies; the normal order lines out there still don't.

Prioritized share for a better price or freight

Filling a truck, or reaching a minimum order value, across one supplier's items

  • First the buffers' own needs: every item at or below TOY gets its order up to TOG (rounded up to whole pallets).
  • Then the top-up: the space left on the truck (or the value still missing for the discount) is added one pallet at a time, always to the item with the lowest planning priority (NFP ÷ TOG) after what is already on the order. Positions end up close to the same percentage, some above TOG.
  • The same top-up fills a container or meets a freight minimum.

Distribution networks

Positioning buffers in a network, and sharing scarce stock

  • Where to buffer: a buffer at the central DC decouples the regions from the supplier's long lead time, so each regional buffer only has to cover the short transfer time. Buffers at the regions let customers be served from stock.
  • Both together give the shortest customer lead time with small regional buffers. Total yellow (ADU × DLT) stays the same; what changes is where it sits: the long lead time is covered once at the central DC, and that stock can still go to whichever region needs it.
  • Prioritized share, too little stock: work zone by zone. First lift every region that is in red just out of red (TOR + 1), then every region just into green (TOY + 1), and only then share the rest so all reach the same planning priority (NFP ÷ TOG). If a stage can't be completed, what is left is shared so the regions it lifts reach the same priority.
  • Prioritized share, too much stock: when the source has made more than the regions need (for example its minimum order quantity was larger), the surplus is pushed out so every region sits at the same percentage above TOG, instead of sitting at the source.
  • Equal % is the simple version (equal NFP ÷ TOG from the start). It gives the same result only when every region's buffer has the same proportions; here North and West have large red zones and South a large green, so the two differ. At 150 units Equal % leaves North in red; at 400 only prioritized share gets every region into green; at 800 the surplus is spread above TOG.
  • Choosing the central location: where most demand is, close to the main suppliers, on good transport lanes, and with room to grow.
  • Several central DCs: in a large network each central DC can stock what is sourced near it and supply the others, with lanes running both ways.
  • Hybrid: fast movers can go straight from the plant to the regions while slow movers go through the central DC. For a fast mover the plant makes one batch for all regions: the sum of every region's TOG − NFP (also those not yet due), at least the plant's minimum, shared out with prioritized share. Try "Fast mover: one plant batch" below.
  • Variability pooling: the central DC sees the sum of the regions' demand. Their ups and downs partly cancel, so the sum varies less relative to its average (lower coefficient of variation) than any single region.

Component 5

Visible and collaborative execution

Once orders are open, priority comes from buffer status, not due date. Planners, buyers and suppliers all see the same signal.

Buffer status alerts

Current on-hand alert
On-hand compared with the red zone today.
Projected on-hand alert
Projected on-hand over the DLT, warning of a red or stockout position before it happens.

Synchronization alerts

Material synchronization alert
A requirement for a part is due before the supply that covers it.
Lead time alert
For lead-time-managed parts (long lead time, not buffered): the last part of the lead time before the due date, the alert horizon, is split into green, yellow and red zones.

Execution priority

Buffer status = On-hand ÷ TOR (%)

  • Redat or below half of TOR
  • Yellowbetween half of TOR and TOR
  • Greenabove TOR

Target on-hand range

Average on-hand target ≈ TOR + Green ÷ 2

On-hand well above TOG means excess. On-hand persistently in red means the buffer is under-sized or supply is late.

Component 6

Tactical adaptation

The final component adjusts the model itself. Past performance (how often buffers ran into red or sat above green, service levels, lead time changes) and planned activity (promotions, seasonality, new products) feed changes to the Master Settings: decoupling points, buffer profiles and planned adjustments.

This review cycle is Demand Driven Sales and Operations Planning (DDS&OP). DDMRP itself is the supply order generation and management engine of the Demand Driven Operating Model (DDOM).

Beyond DDMRP

The Demand Driven Operating Model (DDOM)

  • What it is: the flow-based operating model of a Demand Driven Adaptive Enterprise. It generates supply orders, schedules operations and executes them from actual demand, with no planned orders and no master production schedule [DDI].
  • Four basic elements: paces to actual demand; strategic control points (for scheduling and synchronising resources and orders); strategic decoupling points; and dynamic stock, time and capacity buffers protecting both kinds of point [DDI].
  • Three operating components: DDMRP (supply order generation and management), Demand Driven Capacity Scheduling (resource scheduling) and Demand Driven Execution (order and buffer management in real time) [DDI].
  • Where it sits: DDOM is the operational layer (hours, days, weeks). DDS&OP sets its key parameters, and Adaptive S&OP is the strategic layer above; see the cycle in Component 6 [DDI].
  • Buffer types: stock buffers are the DDMRP buffers at decoupling points; a time buffer protects a control point's schedule by releasing work early; a capacity buffer is spare capacity kept on purpose. DDI's software criteria require tracking orders against time buffer penetration and control point schedules [DDI].

Putting it together

Planning simulation: one buffer, day by day

  • Follow one part for three weeks. Supply orders move toward the buffer from the left (days until receipt); customer demand moves in from the right (today and the next days).
  • Each day: supply due today is received, the planner computes the net flow position and decides on the recommended order, then today's demand and any past-due backlog are shipped from on-hand.
  • Pick a scenario for real-world disturbances: spikes, late or partial deliveries, rejected goods, cancellations, a demand surge, or a planner who skips an order. Use Next day to move on; your decisions (approve, expedite) are replayed when you go back.

Putting it together

Network simulation: DCs, plant and components, day by day

  • Grinder G-10 from the positioning workshop, now as a network of five decoupling points: drive units and blade sets feed the plant's finished-goods buffer, which supplies a North and a South distribution centre.
  • Every node runs the same rules on its own buffer: the DCs order from the plant, the plant's production orders become demand on the two component buffers (decoupled explosion), and the components order from their suppliers. Demand stops at each buffer instead of rippling through the whole network.
  • A production order is released only when both components are in stock; otherwise it waits and a material synchronization alert shows. When the plant can't fill all DC orders, choose how its stock is shared: first come, first served, or prioritized share (both DCs brought to the same planning priority).
  • Pick a scenario and use Next day. The table shows the last five days for all five buffers.
